# Adrenaline (Arousal) Before vs. After Learning

A focused evidence review for LogosDepth, compiled 22 Oct 2026. Companion to `memory-science.md`.

## Limits of this review (same as before, plus one more)

- PubMed/PMC were unreachable from the research environment. All findings come from NCBI-indexed search summaries, so **every number is secondhand**; verify before quoting.
- **Almost no human study measures or manipulates adrenaline (epinephrine) itself.** Researchers raise *arousal* by other means (cold-water stress, emotional videos, shocks, exercise, public-speaking stressors) and infer adrenaline/noradrenaline involvement. The one human epinephrine-infusion study I found (Cahill & Alkire 2003) is known to me only through citations.
- Two search summaries **disagreed** with each other on a key point (whether a change of context helps or kills post-learning stress effects). I flag it below rather than pick one.

Confidence legend: 🟢 strong · 🟡 moderate/mixed · 🔴 weak or contested

---

## The short answer

| Question | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Does a burst of arousal **after** learning help memory? | 🟡 **Probably yes, modestly**, if it comes soon after learning (minutes, up to ~30 min in several studies). The best mechanistic story and the most consistent human evidence in this area. Results are heterogeneous and some well-designed studies found nothing. |
| Does arousal **before** learning help? | 🔴🟡 **Mixed.** Stress before learning generally *impairs* encoding in a meta-analysis, with exceptions. Exercise before learning gives a small average benefit, mostly for free recall. |
| Does arousal at **recall/test time** help? | 🟢 **No. Stress shortly before retrieval tends to impair recall.** |
| Is it a big lever? | 🔴 **No.** Effects are small to moderate and inconsistent; spacing and retrieval practice are far stronger and far better established. |

## 1. Why adrenaline might matter (mechanism)

- **Memory-modulation hypothesis (McGaugh 2004, *Annu Rev Neurosci*; DOI 10.1146/annurev.neuro.27.070203.144157).** After an arousing experience, the amygdala engages adrenergic and glucocorticoid systems that strengthen storage in cortex. Adrenaline is thought to act indirectly (it does not cross the blood-brain barrier well): it stimulates peripheral β-adrenergic receptors, which leads to noradrenaline release in the basolateral amygdala.
- **Blocking it blocks the benefit.** In healthy adults, the β-blocker propranolol before learning removed the extra memory for emotional material (Cahill et al. 1994, *Nature* 371:702) while leaving neutral material roughly unchanged. A [meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3692719/) of propranolol on emotional memory consolidation found a moderate effect (g ≈ 0.44).
- **Glucocorticoids need noradrenaline.** Human and animal work suggests cortisol enhances consolidation mainly when accompanied by noradrenergic arousal ([Roozendaal-style amygdala study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC28430/); [review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28347374/)).
- **Arousal-biased competition and GANE (Mather & Sutherland 2011, [PMC3110019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3110019/)).** Arousal makes processing "winner-take-more": it boosts whatever is already high-priority (salient or goal-relevant) and can suppress low-priority items. This is a major reason arousal effects are inconsistent. They depend on *what* was prioritized.
- **Dose.** An inverted-U (too little or too much is worse) is well supported in animals. In humans it is **debated**, especially for cortisol ([hormetic glucocorticoid review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2657840/)).
- **Animal baseline:** post-training epinephrine injections enhance memory in rats and mice in a dose-dependent way (Gold & van Buskirk 1975; [later work](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1319277)). Human evidence is much thinner.

## 2. Arousal AFTER learning

### The core human findings

- **Epinephrine infusion (Cahill & Alkire 2003; DOI 10.1016/s1074-7427(02)00036-9).** Cited as showing epinephrine given after learning improved human memory consolidation. One review reports 80 ng/kg/min for 3 minutes helped, whereas 40 ng/kg/min and saline did not. *I could not verify the design or sample size.*
- **Post-learning cold-pressor stress (Cahill, Gorski & Le 2003, [PubMed 12888545](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12888545/)).** Stress after viewing slides improved memory for emotionally *arousing* slides but **not neutral ones** in the mixed set.
- **Neutral material specifically:** several studies using *only* neutral word lists find a post-learning benefit:
  - [Nielson et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15890540/): an emotional video shown *after* learning words (unrelated to the words) improved recall at 30 min and 24 h.
  - [Nielson & Powless 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17951081/): positive or negative arousal induced **up to 30 minutes** after learning improved word-list retention.
  - [Nielson et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19285563/): benefit at one week, larger in people predisposed to arousal.
  - [Nociceptive arousal after neutral scenes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6796277/): memory boost appeared only after consolidation and reflected familiarity, not recollection.
- **Meta-analysis (Shields, Sazma, McCullough & Yonelinas 2017, *Psychol Bull*; [PMC5436944](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5436944/)).** Per later summaries, stress **after** encoding generally **improved** memory. I could not retrieve its effect sizes.
- **Time window:** an earlier study found arousal enhanced memory for *preceding* neutral pictures (Anderson, Wais & Gabrieli 2006, [PubMed 16434476](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16434476/)) but **not when arousal came after a longer delay**, consistent with a limited consolidation window.

### The caveats (this is where it gets less tidy)

- **Failures to replicate.** A [socially evaluated stressor after learning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7766306/) raised cortisol and anxiety but did **not** change 24-hour recall of emotional or neutral pictures. [Another study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7349384/) found a cortisol rise *hurt* memory for contextual elements. A [review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4096032/) concludes the stress–consolidation relationship is "bounded," depends on arousal, hormone profile and timing.
- **Type of memory.** One cold-pressor study found the benefit for **familiarity-based recognition, not recollection**, in men ([PubMed 23823181](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23823181/)). Verse recitation is recall, not recognition.
- **Context is unresolved (🔴).** One summary of the meta-analysis says the post-encoding benefit shrinks or vanishes when the stressor happens in a *different* context from studying. A different summary of a 2024 study ([PMC10909400](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10909400/)) says stress helped *only* when context changed. These conflict; I couldn't resolve them. Don't design anything around context shifts yet.
- **Sex.** Several individual studies found larger effects in men; the meta-analysis reportedly found sex was *not* a reliable moderator. Mixed.
- **Age.** [Arousal-enhanced memory shrinks with age](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10509193/) (largest for negative material), and arousal improved binding in younger but not older adults ([study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3058756/)).
- **Rodents often show *impairment* from post-encoding stress**, depending on habituation to the context.

### The exercise route (a practical way to raise adrenaline)

Moderate exercise raises epinephrine (and, less so, norepinephrine); that is the working explanation for these results, but **it is a hypothesis in humans**, not a demonstrated mediator.

- **Exercise 4 hours after learning, not immediately,** improved retention in [van Dongen et al. 2016 (*Curr Biol*)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7957845/) (via later summaries). Immediate exercise had no effect there.
- **But other work found benefits from immediate exercise.** [Makepeace & Craig 2024 (PLOS One)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39269940/): in endurance runners, 25 minutes of **higher-intensity** interval running right after learning words beat lower-intensity walking at both 30 min and 24 h (a within-subject field study; treat as suggestive). [Another study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5569643/) reports benefit from just 5 minutes of immediate post-encoding exercise in women.
- **A December 2025 narrative review** ([PMC12842734](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12842734/)) argues moderate aerobic exercise **within ~30 minutes after learning** helps via an epinephrine rise. That "30 min" comes from the authors' synthesis, not a single pooled trial.
- **Pooled evidence:** an acute-exercise meta-analysis reported **exercise after encoding d ≈ 0.33**, before encoding d ≈ 0.23, during encoding about 0 (summary attributed to Qazi et al., [PMC13281107](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13281107/); a separate [temporal meta-analysis, PMC6523402](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6523402/), found slightly negative effects for exercise *during* encoding). The optimal timing "remains unknown."
- **Animals:** rats' exercise-enhanced memory is abolished by blocking noradrenergic or dopaminergic receptors ([PubMed 42302961](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42302961/)), supporting the catecholamine story, though exercise there came right after training.

## 3. Arousal BEFORE learning

- **Meta-analysis (Shields et al. 2017).** Per summaries: stress **before or during** encoding generally **impaired** episodic memory, except when the gap between stressor and learning was very short *and* the material was related to the stressor. The impairing threshold is reported as roughly 17–22 minutes between stress and encoding. Sources disagree on the exact cutoff, so I don't trust it.
- **Counter-example for neutral words:** [Schwabe et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18334304/): cold-pressor stress *before* learning enhanced recall of **neutral** (and emotional) words, and for neutral words this did not depend on the cortisol response. A [review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4096032/) notes other neutral-stimulus studies were mixed and suggests noradrenergic arousal from emotional content may be key.
- **Nearby arousing events can impair *neutral* items** or help them (retrograde enhancement vs. impairment) depending on material and timing ([review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2917000/)); negative arousal impaired associative memory for neutral content in [one study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4505890/).
- **Exercise before learning:** small pooled benefit (d ≈ 0.23), concentrated in **free recall (d ≈ 0.40)**, with ~0 for cued recall (0.08) and slightly negative for recognition (−0.06) (summary of the same acute-exercise meta-analysis; I could not confirm which paper). The proposed mechanism is better attention at encoding, which is not specifically adrenergic. Light-to-moderate activity *during* learning helped vocabulary in [one study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23700461/), mostly in initially low performers, with BDNF ruled out as the mechanism.

## 4. Arousal at recall

- A [systematic review of 13 studies (N = 962)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33084805/) and the Shields meta-analysis agree: **acute stress shortly before retrieval impairs recall** (typically a lab stressor 15–45 min earlier). Evidence that cortisol is the cause is mixed. So test-day nerves are a handicap, not a booster.

---

## 5. Adjacent: caffeine (not adrenaline, but a common "stimulant" question)

- [Borota et al. 2014 (*Nat Neurosci*)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24473261/): 200 mg caffeine right **after** studying improved next-day discrimination between similar items, *not* general recognition. A [2025 face-recognition study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12267838) following that design found **no** benefit. Weak, narrow, unreplicated.

---

## 6. What this means for memorizing scripture

1. **It's an add-on, not a method.** Pooled arousal/exercise effects (d ≈ 0.2–0.4) are smaller and less reliable than retrieval practice (g ≈ 0.5) and spaced repetition. Build the scheduler first.
2. **Best-supported, lowest-risk use:** a short bout of brisk-to-vigorous activity soon after a study session (the post-learning window), plus doing the first retest a day later. Several studies, one plausible mechanism. Not proven for verse memorization.
3. **Don't induce stress.** Avoid anything that makes users anxious before they recall: harsh timers, penalties, public leaderboards at test time. Stress before retrieval impairs recall.
4. **Priority matters.** Arousal amplifies what's already important to the learner. Letting users tag a verse as personally meaningful (and using verses with real emotional salience) fits arousal-biased competition and the self-reference literature.
5. **Run N-of-1 experiments in the app.** Because the evidence is heterogeneous, let each user test it on themselves: alternate study days with and without a post-session movement break, compare next-day typed recall. That turns an uncertain literature into personal data. It requires the review scheduler and typed-recall step first.

**Safety note (not medical advice):** do not attempt pharmacological adrenaline or self-administered stressors; the infusion work was done in controlled lab settings. People with heart conditions, or on β-blockers (which blunt this pathway), should check with a clinician before vigorous exercise.

## Not verified / gaps

- Effect sizes from Shields et al. (2017), Cahill & Alkire (2003), and the acute-exercise meta-analyses (which paper reports which number).
- The context-shift contradiction in §2.
- No study found directly testing arousal timing for **verse or prose memorization**, nor for **verbatim recall** (most use pictures, short word lists, or recognition).
- Not searched: heart-rate-variability, breathing exercises, cold exposure, music-induced arousal, individual-difference moderators beyond sex and age.
