# The Science of Memorizing Scripture

An evidence review for LogosDepth, compiled 22 Oct 2026.

## Read this first: what this is and isn't

**It is** a targeted review of the strongest PubMed/PMC-indexed evidence (meta-analyses, systematic reviews, landmark experiments) on how people memorize verbal material such as verses, and what to build from it.

**It is not** a review of "all PubMed papers." PubMed holds hundreds of thousands of memory records; no one can read them all, and this is not a PRISMA-style systematic review.

**Important limits on the evidence trail:**

- PubMed, PMC, Europe PMC and Crossref were **unreachable** from the research environment (blocked by the sandbox network policy). I could not open a single abstract directly.
- Everything below was gathered through web searches restricted to `ncbi.nlm.nih.gov`. **Every effect size is therefore secondhand**: taken from search-result summaries or from later papers citing the original. Treat numbers as pointers, and check the linked record before quoting them.
- Where I only have a DOI (no PubMed link surfaced), I give the DOI.
- See **`sources.md`** for the source registry: independent corroboration for each core claim, source-credibility tiers, corrections to this document, and a short list of primary papers to read in full.
- Topics **not** searched: nutrition, working-memory training, interference/reconsolidation theory in depth, multimedia/cognitive-load design, bilingual effects.
- No study I found tested **Bible-verse memorization** directly (see "Scripture-specific evidence").

**Confidence legend:** 🟢 strong and replicated · 🟡 moderate or mixed · 🔴 weak, indirect or contested · ⛔ not supported

## TL;DR: ten rules, ranked by strength of evidence

| # | Rule | Confidence |
|---|------|-----------|
| 1 | **Retrieve, don't re-read.** Trying to recall beats studying again, and the gap grows with delay. | 🟢 |
| 2 | **Space it out.** Spread practice over days and weeks; cramming only wins for tests minutes away. | 🟢 |
| 3 | **Relearn across sessions until recall succeeds repeatedly** (successive relearning), then keep reviewing spaced out over time (equal and expanding schedules perform about the same). | 🟢 |
| 4 | **Keep practice winnable.** Retrieval helps most when you succeed often (~50%+), and when feedback follows errors. | 🟡🟢 |
| 5 | **Produce, don't just recognize.** Generating or saying the words beats selecting them from a list (format evidence is mixed, see below). | 🟡 |
| 6 | **Chunk into phrases** and work within ~4 chunks at a time. | 🟡 |
| 7 | **Sleep after learning; don't skimp on sleep.** | 🟡 |
| 8 | **Make it meaningful and personal** (elaboration, self-reference), but still test the *exact* wording if exact wording is the goal. | 🟡 |
| 9 | **Rewards should follow successful recall, not time spent.** Gamification helps on average but is heterogeneous, and rewards can backfire. | 🟡 |
| 10 | **Don't rely on feelings of knowing.** Learners systematically pick low-yield strategies; the app should do the scheduling. | 🟢 |

## Evidence by technique

### 1. Retrieval practice (the "testing effect") 🟢

- **Landmark experiments.** Roediger & Karpicke (2006, *Psychological Science*; DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x): students studied prose passages once and were tested repeatedly, or re-read repeatedly. Re-reading won on a 5-minute test; **testing won on a 1-week test**. This reversal is the single most important pattern in the field.
- **Karpicke & Roediger (2008, *Science*)**, ["The critical importance of retrieval for learning"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18276894/): after Swahili–English pairs were first recalled correctly, further *retrieval* kept recall at about **80% after one week**, versus about **33–36%** when pairs were only *restudied* (figures from secondary summaries; **I could not re-confirm them**). What I did confirm about the design: 40 students, 40 word pairs, 8 alternating study/test periods, and cumulative learning at the end of training was nearly identical across conditions, so the gap only appeared on delayed tests.
- **Meta-analysis.** Rowland (2014, *Psychological Bulletin*; DOI 10.1037/a0037559) is widely cited for a reliable medium effect, **g ≈ 0.50**, for testing over restudy. Per later summaries: gains are larger at longer delays, shrink when success on practice tests is low (below ~50%), and are boosted by feedback (delayed feedback somewhat more). The abstract itself reports that **initial recall tests produced larger testing benefits than recognition tests**, which supports typed recall over tapping from a word bank. Secondary sources put the sample at 159 effect sizes from 61 studies, and one lower-credibility summary site reports feedback roughly doubling the effect (g ≈ 0.73 with vs. 0.39 without).
- **Breadth.** Adesope et al. (2017, *Review of Educational Research*; DOI 10.3102/0034654316689306) is the other major meta-analysis of practice testing. A [systematic review of 56 studies in health-professions education](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11078833/) found distributed and retrieval practice often improved grades, but heterogeneity prevented pooling.
- **Dunlosky et al. (2013)**, [PubMed 26173288](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/), rated **practice testing and distributed practice "high utility"**, and **summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, and re-reading "low utility"**; elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaving "moderate".

**Caveats worth knowing**

- A [2023 PMC re-examination](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10783554/) argues Karpicke & Blunt's (2011, *Science*) win over concept mapping may be partly a **methodological artifact** (concept-mapping participants were told not to try to memorize). The testing effect vs. *restudy* is much better established than vs. *elaborative* strategies.
- A [pre-registered word-learning study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5545031/) failed to replicate the classic effect under some conditions.
- Retrieval is effortful. A [study of working-memory capacity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37002242/) reports it is **beneficial mainly when working-memory resources are abundant**, with low-capacity learners and hard-to-link material benefiting less. Implication for beginners: *scaffold, then fade.*
- A [small meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36760059/) found retrieval practice **hurt transitive inference** (d ≈ -0.37). It helps recall; it's not a universal win for reasoning.

### 2. Spacing and scheduling 🟢

- **Cepeda et al. (2006)**, ["Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/): review of **839 assessments in 317 experiments from 184 articles**. Spacing beats massing; all studies with retention intervals over a month showed a clear distributed-practice benefit (per secondary summary).
- **Cepeda et al. (2008)**, ["A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/): **>1,350 learners**. The optimal gap rises with how long you need to remember, but shrinks as a *proportion*: roughly **20–40% of a 1-week delay, falling to ~5–10% of a 1-year delay**. Too short a gap costs more than too long a gap (inverted-U).
- **Pashler et al. (2007)**, [PubMed 17694899](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17694899), covers spacing over retention intervals up to a year.
- **Big-data confirmation:** ["The spacing effect stands up to big data"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30623389/).
- **Forgetting curve:** Murre & Dros (2015, *PLOS ONE*; DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0120644) replicated Ebbinghaus's curve, but this was a **single-subject** study (one participant, about 70 hours of learning and relearning), and I found no independent replication. Treat it as a confirmation of the general shape of forgetting, not as population data.
- **Spaced repetition in practice:** a [meta-analysis in medical education](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41601436/) reports an overall benefit (**SMD ≈ 0.78, ~21,415 learners, 13 studies pooled**; per search summary). A [systematic review of Anki use](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42183420/) found a modest, consistent link to better exam scores **but no randomized assignment**, so self-selection may explain part of it.
- **Adaptive scheduling:** [Tabibian et al. 2019 (PNAS)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30670661/) derived a scheduling algorithm (MEMORIZE) from optimal-control theory and validated it on Duolingo data; a [later randomized experiment (~50,700 learners)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8421401) reported ML-scheduled instruction helped people memorize more effectively. (I only saw summaries of both.)

### 3. Successive relearning 🟢

- [Rawson, Vaughn, Walsh & Dunlosky (2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29431462/): relearning across multiple sessions gave **large retention gains** over single-session learning, and the effects of how you first learned **shrank after relearning** ("relearning override").
- [Rawson & Dunlosky (2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23088488/): relearning attenuates both the benefits and costs of initial spacing.
- **Take-away:** one successful session is *not* mastery. Mastery is repeated success on separate days.

### 4. Pretesting / prequestions 🟡

- Failed guesses *before* learning still help later encoding: [Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/); [Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19751074/).
- A preregistered [meta-analysis of the prequestion effect](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37640836/): **specific effect g ≈ 0.54 (k = 97)** (gains on the questioned content), **general effect g ≈ 0.04 (k = 91)** (essentially none on unquestioned content).
- Pretesting vs. posttesting on text passages favored pretesting up to 48 h in one study (Pan & Sana 2021, DOI 10.1037/xap0000345), with no difference same-day.
- Learners underestimate how well errorful learning works.

### 5. Feedback 🟡🟢

- Feedback strengthens the testing effect; per Rowland, delayed feedback may give a bigger boost.
- [Hypercorrection](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079415/): confident errors are often *better* corrected, probably via surprise, but evidence is mostly single lab studies.
- [Feedback-type study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9293038/): corrected memories were **more easily forgotten between 5 minutes and 7 days**, so corrections need re-testing, not just showing.

### 6. Interspersing retrieval while learning ("grain size") 🟡

- A [meta-analysis of 19 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39556402/): retrieval **interspersed during learning** beat the same retrieval **at the end**, **g ≈ 0.25 [0.09, 0.42]**. For verses: quiz yourself after each phrase, not only after the whole verse.

### 7. Encoding strategies

| Strategy | Confidence | What the evidence says |
|---|---|---|
| **Reading aloud / production effect** | 🟡🟢 | Robust for words (MacLeod et al. 2010 coined the term; [special issue intro](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27244350/)). [Production benefits learning and endures for text](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22827717/). But [reading text aloud benefits memory, *not comprehension*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37440162/), and the effect is reported to be smaller in recognition tasks when produced and unproduced items are in separate lists. Fawcett's (2013) meta-analysis is titled as showing the production effect also holds in between-subject designs. |
| **Generation effect** | 🟡 | Robust meta-analytically (Bertsch et al. 2007, DOI 10.3758/BF03193441), but a [replication attempt on expository text](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40549267/) found no effect in most of 7 experiments; it is most likely with intentional learning, unrestricted time, immediate tests. |
| **Self-reference / elaboration** | 🟡🟢 | [Symons & Johnson (1997)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9136641/), **129 studies**: self-referential encoding beat semantic and other-person encoding, attributed to elaboration and organization. |
| **Chunking & working-memory limits** | 🟡 | Working memory holds roughly **~4 chunks** ([Cowan 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/)); the exact number is debated (up to ~7 depending on method). Meaningful chunking is how people exceed raw limits. |
| **Method of loci (memory palace)** | 🟡🔴 | [Dresler et al. 2017, *Neuron*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28279356/): 23 memory athletes vs. controls; **6 weeks** of training in naive controls reshaped brain networks toward athletes' patterns and predicted gains up to 4 months. A [2025 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40457944/): **d ≈ 0.88 [0.47, 1.25]** vs. rehearsal for **immediate serial recall**, only 13 effect sizes pooled, **certainty rated low to very low**. |
| **Keyword mnemonic / imagery for text** | 🔴 | Rated **low utility** by Dunlosky et al. 2013. |
| **Gestures / enactment** | 🟡🔴 | A [meta-analysis on foreign-vocabulary learning](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10669578/) (7 studies, 309 people) found *observing* gestures about as good as *performing* them, with wide variability. Meaningful (iconic) gestures help more than meaningless ones. Narrative-recall results are mixed. |
| **Music / melody** | 🔴 | [Effects of music on memory for text](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18712216/) is mixed; in one study melody did not improve recall of lyrics, whereas [spaced practice clearly helped lyric retention](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8665960/). **Background music with lyrics worsened** verbal/visual memory tasks ([study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10162369/)). |
| **Handwriting vs. typing** | 🔴 | EEG shows connectivity differences ([van der Weel & van der Meer 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10853352/)), but a [commentary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11750765/) notes the study measured no learning. No solid recall advantage established. |
| **Interleaving** | 🟡 | Brunmair & Richter (2019; DOI 10.1037/bul0000209): medium average benefit, strongest for **similar, hard-to-discriminate** categories; smaller for math. Unclear for unrelated verses. |

### 8. Retrieval format: recognition vs. recall 🟡

- A [university study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30564174) found a testing effect for **short-answer** but not **multiple-choice** practice; a [2024 head-to-head](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11684041/) found **no difference**. Kang, McDermott & Roediger (2007) is the usual citation for format effects.
- **Honest summary:** typed or spoken recall is *probably* at least as good and often better than picking from a bank, and it is a better rehearsal for real-life recall. But the evidence is not decisive.

### 9. Consolidation: sleep

- **Sleep 🟡.** Diekelmann & Born (2010, *Nat Rev Neurosci*; DOI 10.1038/nrn2762) and Klinzing, Niethard & Born (2019, *Nat Neurosci*; DOI 10.1038/s41593-019-0467-3) are the main reviews of "active systems consolidation". A [meta-analysis of sleep deprivation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8893218/) found a small harm after learning (**g ≈ 0.28**), larger on immediate tests and for procedural tasks. An [age meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28161367/) found sleep-based benefits in young but **not** older adults. A [recent study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42165620/) found wake selectively hurt *weakly* learned items. Nocturnal sleep may beat naps for related pairs ([study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4168137/)). I found no pooled sleep-vs-wake effect size for declarative memory.

### 10. Motivation and game design

- **Gamification 🟡.** A [broad meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10591086/) (41 studies, ~5,071 participants) reported **g ≈ 0.82** on learning outcomes, **but only ≈ 0.01 in higher education**, and rigor is uneven. Sailer & Homner (2020) is cited for a more conservative **g ≈ 0.49 cognitive (95% CI [0.30, 0.69], k = 19), 0.36 motivational, 0.25 behavioral**; the cognitive effect held up in the high-rigor subset, the other two were less stable. A [review of gamified cognitive training](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7445616/) found games were *more motivating* (g = 0.72) but produced **no gains on the trained domain**.
- **Reward 🟡🔴.** [Adcock et al. 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16675403/): reward anticipation engages midbrain–hippocampus circuits and predicts later memory (dopamine inferred, not measured). But [one word-learning study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17390317/) found external rewards can have **null or even negative** effects.
- **Curiosity 🟡.** [Gruber et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/): high-curiosity states improved memory, even for incidental items shown during them.

### 11. Metacognition: why learners need a system 🟢

- Learners' judgments do not reflect the testing benefit (Karpicke & Roediger 2008). In [Karpicke 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19883131), when students controlled their own learning, many **dropped items instead of practicing retrieval**, hurting retention. Therefore: **the app should schedule reviews and prompt recall rather than leave strategy to the learner.**

### 12. Myths ⛔

- **Learning styles / "matching" instruction.** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008; DOI 10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x) found no adequate evidence for the matching hypothesis. Don't build "visual learner / auditory learner" modes; offer modalities (read, hear, say, write) to everyone.
- **Re-reading and highlighting** feel productive but are low utility.

## Scripture-specific evidence: what exists

- **No trials of Bible-verse memorization methods were found.** The principles above are general-memory findings applied to scripture, which is a reasonable inference, not a demonstrated result.
- **Quran / Vedic memorization is observational.** [Association between scripture memorization and brain atrophy (n = 63)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32214278/): complete Quran memorizers had more grey/white matter than controls, but this cannot show causation. [Vedic priests (~17,700 training hours)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8102627/) show structural differences, also an association. A [systematic review of Quran listening/recitation/memorization](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9470760/) calls benefits suggestive, not conclusive. **Do not promise users health or brain benefits.**
- **Memory for text is partly gist-based.** [Repertory actors](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11747573/) recalled a script >5 months later at about **85%**, but a substantial share (**~32%**) of output was **paraphrase or inference** rather than verbatim, suggesting meaning-based learning. For scripture, where exact wording often matters, test the **exact words** explicitly.

## Audit: how the current game measures up

| Current feature | Evidence says | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Read → fill → order → recall in one sitting | Within-session success is not durable learning; benefits show up at delay | 🟡 Good scaffold, but **not mastery** |
| **No spaced reviews** | Spacing and successive relearning are the two strongest levers | 🔴 **Biggest gap** |
| 3 stars after a single session = "mastered" | Mastery should mean repeated recall on separate days | 🔴 Misleading |
| Word-bank fill-in | Recognition; recall format probably better, evidence mixed | 🟡 Add typed recall |
| Progressive blanking (25% → 70%) | Aligns with "scaffold then fade" for low-capacity beginners | 🟢 Keep |
| Phrase chunks in Order step | Aligns with chunking (~4 chunks); long verses may exceed it | 🟡 Cap chunk count |
| Errors cost nothing except stars; hints available | Keeps success rate high, which retrieval needs | 🟢 Keep |
| XP awarded for finishing steps (even with hints) | Rewards should track successful recall | 🟡 Rework |
| Link to Hebrew/Greek words | Elaboration, a meaning link | 🟢 Keep |
| Listen button (text-to-speech) | Hearing is fine, but *producing* aloud is what the production effect measures | 🟡 Add "say it aloud" |
| Single "Word Game" quiz uses multiple choice | Recognition format | 🟡 Fine for vocabulary; not for verses |

## Proposed build order (my synthesis, not a validated protocol)

**Status (22 Oct 2026):** items 1, 2, 3 and 8 are implemented (`static/js/recall.js`, `static/js/verses.js`; unit tests in `tests/test_recall.js`, browser tests in `tests/e2e.py`). Design choices that are **not** evidence-based and should be revisited: the interval ladder (1, 3, 7, 14, 30, 60, 120 days), the spelling leniency in typed grading (longer words may have one or two letter slips; words of five letters or fewer must match exactly), and the XP amounts. Items 4–7, 9 and 10 are not built.

1. **Spaced review scheduler (highest impact).** After a verse is learned, queue reviews spaced over days to weeks (e.g., next day, ~3 days, ~1 week, ~2 weeks, ~1 month; reset the interval after a miss). A 2021 meta-analysis found spaced retrieval beats massed retrieval (g ≈ 0.74) but **no clear advantage for expanding over equal spacing**, so don't over-engineer the interval pattern; real spacing and a genuine recall attempt at each review are what matter. The Cepeda ridgeline supports gaps that are a fraction of the target retention horizon; the exact schedule is a design choice and should be tuned and ideally A/B tested. A daily "Today's review" page, interleaving due verses.
2. **Typed (or spoken) recall step.** Final step: show the reference and first words (or first letters of each word), learner types the verse; grade leniently on spelling/punctuation, strictly on word order; always show the exact text afterward.
3. **Redefine mastery.** 3 stars = successful unaided recall on ≥ 3 separate days (per successive relearning), with the original session counting as day 1.
4. **Phrase-level retrieval while learning.** Insert a quick recall check after each phrase (grain-size effect), then build up: phrase 1, 1+2, 1+2+3…, capping each working set around 4 chunks.
5. **Say-it-aloud prompts** at the Read step and before typed recall (production effect), with optional speech recognition as a later feature.
6. **Pretest option:** "Guess the next phrase before you read it" for verses the user half-knows (the prequestion effect is specific to the content asked about).
7. **Meaning prompt:** one optional "put it in your own words / what does this mean for you?" before recall (self-reference, elaboration), plus always testing the verbatim text.
8. **XP redesign:** award XP for *delayed* successful recalls and for streak of review days; reduce XP for hint-assisted passes; keep rewards modest to avoid crowding out intrinsic motivation.
9. **Evening review nudge** (sleep consolidation, low confidence; frame as a tip, not a claim).
10. **Advanced mode (later):** method-of-loci coaching for memorizing the *order* of many verses or long passages (the evidence base is promising but low-certainty).

**Deliberately not recommended:** learning-style modes, highlighting/re-read-only modes, background-music features, health/brain-benefit claims, or "melody makes it stick" claims.

## What I could not verify

- Every number above (effect sizes, sample sizes) came from search summaries, **not** abstracts. Verify before citing publicly.
- Roediger & Karpicke 2006, Rowland 2014, Adesope 2017, Brunmair & Richter 2019, Murayama et al. 2014 (retrieval-induced forgetting), Bertsch 2007, Pashler 2008, and Kang et al. 2007 are given by DOI because no PubMed link surfaced in my searches. Retrieval-induced forgetting (practicing some items can impair related unpracticed ones) is robust per citing papers but its *mechanism* is debated, so keep related verses in the same review rotation.
- Some records returned by search carry 2025–2026 dates; I couldn't independently check them.
